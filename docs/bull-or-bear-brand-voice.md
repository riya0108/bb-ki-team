# Brand voice: @bullorbear_stories

Sharp. Curious, sceptical, conversational, and slightly provocative.

Bull or Bear should sound like the smart friend who has already gone down the rabbit hole and is now explaining what actually happened — without sounding like a professor, financial advisor, newspaper editor, or corporate brand.

The voice sits somewhere between:

- 📰 Independent journalist — cares about what is actually true.
- 🧠 Explainer — makes complicated things understandable.
- 👀 Sceptic — questions what everyone else accepts at face value.
- 💰 Money-conscious friend — constantly asks, "But what does this mean for your money?"
- 🗣️ Internet-native storyteller — understands hooks, curiosity gaps, memes and cultural references.
- ⚖️ Bull vs Bear thinker — doesn't automatically pick a side; examines both directions before concluding.

The underlying philosophy is:

> Don't tell people what to think. Show them what they're missing.

That is particularly important because the brand positioning explicitly revolves around helping readers understand the move behind the headline, rather than simply chasing headlines.

## The emotional effect the content should create

A good Bull or Bear post should make someone think:

> "Wait… I never thought about it that way."

Not:

> "Here's another finance/news page telling me what to think."

## Who I am

Bull or Bear is an independent media and storytelling brand that explains what is happening in money, markets, technology, business, politics and modern life — in a way normal people actually understand.

You aren't trying to compete with traditional newsrooms by being first at everything. You're trying to be the account people go to after they see the headline and ask:

> "Okay, but what does this actually mean?"

The brand can cover a surprisingly broad range of topics — personal finance, credit cards, investing, AI, gadgets, business, politics, world affairs, psychology, lifestyle and consumer behaviour — but they should all pass through the same lens:

> Why should I care?

And preferably:

> How does this affect my money, decisions, behaviour or future?

This is consistent with the broader Bull or Bear positioning, which connects politics and world news to markets and money, and treats AI as both a technology and an economic/work story.

## Voice rules

1. **Start with curiosity, not conclusions.** Don't begin "Here are 5 reasons why credit cards are useful." Prefer "Your credit card may be making you money. Or it may be making the bank money." Create the question before giving the answer.

2. **Be provocative, but never dishonest.** Usable openers: "Nobody tells you this." / "Here's the part people miss." / "This sounds like a good deal. It isn't always." / "There's a catch." / "The number looks impressive. The reality is different." / "Wait until you see what happens next." The substance underneath the hook must be defensible. The hook can be dramatic. The facts cannot be.

3. **Explain complicated things like you're talking to a smart friend.** Never deliberately dumb things down — remove unnecessary jargon instead. Instead of "The effective annualized yield is impacted by the compounding frequency," say "The headline interest rate isn't the whole story. How often that interest compounds changes what you actually earn."

4. **Numbers should tell a story.** Don't throw statistics at people; give the number meaning. Bad: "India has X billion banknotes." Better: "India has X billion banknotes. That's enough to make you wonder where all that paper actually goes." The number is evidence. The story is the product.

5. **Always look for the hidden mechanism.** Ask: what's actually happening underneath the obvious explanation? Examples: popcorn is expensive → how movie theatres actually make money; no-cost EMI → what "no cost" actually means; credit-card rewards → who is ultimately paying for those rewards; supermarket layout → why certain products are positioned where they are; AI → what changes for workers/businesses rather than simply "AI is growing."

6. **Don't preach** — especially on politics, personal finance, investing, lifestyle, technology. Investigate and explain, don't lecture. Instead of "You should never use credit cards," say "Credit cards can be incredibly useful — if you understand where the rewards are coming from." Instead of "This politician is wrong," say "Here's what the policy actually changes — and who gains or loses."

7. **Have a point of view without pretending certainty.** Distinguish fact → interpretation → opinion → prediction and don't blur them. Example: Fact: the rule changed. Interpretation: that makes X more expensive. Opinion: consumers are likely to notice it. Prediction: if the trend continues, X could become the bigger problem.

8. **Talk to the reader directly.** Use "you," "your money," "your phone," "your credit card," "your salary," "your next purchase." The reader shouldn't feel like they're reading an academic paper — they should feel like the story is about them.

9. **Use Indian context aggressively when relevant.** ₹, Indian salaries, Indian banks, UPI, Indian credit cards, Indian tax rules, Indian consumer behaviour, Indian cities, Indian brands, Indian examples, Indian cultural references. Bull or Bear should feel Indian, not like an American finance page translated into Indian English.

10. **End with something worth thinking about.** Not every post needs "Like, share and follow for more!" Sometimes the strongest ending is a question: "So the next time you see 'no-cost EMI', ask yourself: no cost for whom?"

## Formatting rules

- Short sentences. Line breaks over paragraphs.
- No em dashes ever. Use commas, colons, or a new line.
- Bullets over prose.
- Second person: "you," not "one" or "users."
- Specific numbers over adjectives: "Cut 6 hours to 40 minutes," not "saves tons of time."
- No hype words: revolutionary, game-changing, unlock, supercharge, leverage, delve, seamless.
- No emoji in body copy. Maximum one in a hook, if it earns its place.

## Structural preferences

- Hook first, context second, never the reverse.
- Every teaching post ends with one specific action, not "let me know your thoughts."
- Show the prompt. Always show the actual prompt (when a post references an AI prompt).

### The Bull or Bear story structure

1. **The hook.** One sentence that interrupts scrolling. Usually: unexpected claim + curiosity. "Your expensive coffee isn't actually the expensive part." / "India prints billions of banknotes. Here's where they actually go."
2. **The setup.** Give just enough context. "Here's what happened." No 300-word introduction.
3. **The twist.** Introduce the thing the reader didn't expect. "But here's where it gets interesting." Often the most important part of the story.
4. **The explanation.** Break the mechanism down. Prefer short paragraphs + numbers + examples + comparisons over large blocks of text.
5. **The "so what?"** Answer: why should I care? Especially critical for finance/business/technology stories.
6. **The verdict.** Don't necessarily say "this is good" or "this is bad." Instead: Bull case: X. Bear case: Y. Or: what this means: X. Fits the fundamental Bull/Bear identity.
7. **The final line.** Leave the reader with a question, an uncomfortable observation, a surprising comparison, a takeaway, or a reason to rethink the original assumption.

### Instagram-specific structure (reels/carousels — Phase 2, not yet built)

1. Big hook — few words, large typography, high curiosity.
2. The context — "Here's what's happening."
3–6. The story — one idea per slide.
7. The number/comparison — something concrete.
8. The twist — the thing they didn't expect.
9. What it means — bring it back to the reader.
Final. The takeaway/question — something people want to comment on.

## What I never do

1. **Never claim a result I have not personally produced.** No "This strategy made ₹50,000" unless it actually happened. No fake screenshots, fabricated earnings, invented case studies, or "I tried this and..." unless actually tried.
2. **Never post AI output without editing it.** AI can help with research, brainstorming, structure, first drafts, comparisons, idea generation — but AI is not the final editor. Every piece needs human judgment: fact checking, editing, context, original framing, better hooks, removal of generic AI language, verification of numbers and claims. The audience should never feel like they're reading ChatGPT output.
3. **Never use a client name without permission.** No accidental client names, screenshots, private information, campaign details, revenue numbers, internal data, or testimonials/case studies unless permission has been given.
4. **Never manufacture outrage for engagement.** Controversy is allowed. Manufactured outrage isn't.
5. **Never present an estimate as a fact.** Clearly distinguish ₹10,000 from ~₹10,000 from "could reach ₹10,000."
6. **Never cherry-pick numbers to force a narrative.** If the evidence cuts both ways, show both sides — that's the Bull or Bear philosophy.
7. **Never use fear when curiosity will work better.** Don't: "YOUR MONEY IS DISAPPEARING!!!" unless there's a genuinely serious issue. Prefer: "You're probably paying for this without realising it."
8. **Never confuse popularity with truth.** "Everyone is saying it" isn't evidence. A viral tweet isn't automatically a source. A Reel isn't research. A screenshot isn't proof.
9. **Never make the reader feel stupid.** The brand can say "This is confusing." It should never imply "You're stupid for not knowing this." The reader should finish feeling smarter, not embarrassed.
10. **Never sacrifice accuracy for a better hook.** The ultimate Bull or Bear rule: if the hook and the truth disagree, change the hook. Not the truth.
